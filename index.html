What “survive all seasons” means

A plant marked Yes in the A–Z section can generally remain indoors year-round in a normal home if its light, temperature and watering needs are met. It does not mean it grows at the same speed all year.

Rule of thumb: don't water because the calendar says it is watering day. Check the pot/soil first. Many houseplants suffer more from excess water than from a slightly late drink.

How to make new plants from old plants

Propagation is one of the best parts of owning plants: one healthy plant can become a collection, gifts for friends, or plants for every room.

Choose healthy material

Pick a healthy, pest-free stem. For stem cuttings, a piece around 8–10 cm with 2–3 leaves is a useful starting point. Clean your scissors or secateurs first.

Cut just below a node

The node is where a leaf joins the stem. Remove the lower leaves so the node can sit in water or propagation mix.

Root it in water or mix

Easy plants such as pothos, tradescantia and many philodendrons can root in water. Alternatively use a free-draining propagation mix.

Bright, warm, but not scorching

Place cuttings in bright indirect light. Avoid strong direct sun while roots are forming. Keep the medium moist, not saturated.

Wait for roots

Many easy stem cuttings can root in roughly 3–4 weeks, though species vary. Water-rooted cuttings should have a useful root system before potting up.

Pot into a small container

Use a small pot with drainage. Don't jump straight into a huge pot: excess wet mix around a small root system can encourage rot.

🌱 Lawn & Garden Care

A new-house section for getting the lawn established and keeping the garden healthy through the year. The exact mowing and watering rhythm depends on your grass species, soil, drainage and Melbourne weather.

First identify your lawn

Common warm-season lawns include buffalo, couch and kikuyu. Cool-season grasses such as fescue and ryegrass behave differently. If you are unsure, take a close photo of the blades and use a plant-identification app or nursery/garden-centre advice before changing fertiliser or watering.

Simple lawn checklist

Check the soil

Compacted soil, poor drainage and unsuitable pH can cause problems that fertiliser will not fix. A soil test is useful before major amendments.

Water deeply

Watering should encourage a useful root system rather than keeping the top few millimetres permanently wet. Follow local water restrictions and your grass's needs.

Mow little and often

Avoid removing a huge amount of leaf in one cut. Sharp blades give a cleaner cut and reduce ragged leaf damage.

Feed based on need

Use a fertiliser appropriate to the grass and season. More fertiliser is not automatically better; excessive nutrients can cause weak growth and runoff.

Repair bare spots

Find the cause first — shade, traffic, drainage, pests or poor soil — then seed, patch or re-turf at the appropriate time for your grass.

For a Melbourne home, the next useful upgrade would be a dedicated lawn diagnostic: enter your grass type, lawn size, hours of sun and irrigation setup, and the bible can generate a seasonal mowing/watering/feeding checklist.

The Watering Bible

The best watering schedule is usually not a schedule. Light, temperature, pot size, pot material, soil mix, plant size and season all change how quickly a plant drinks. Use the calendar as a reminder to check the plant.

💧 The 5-step watering method

Check before watering

Push a finger into the potting mix. For many tropical foliage plants, check roughly the upper 2–5 cm. Succulents and drought-tolerant plants generally need substantially more drying between drinks.

Learn the pot's weight

Lift smaller pots after watering and again when dry. A dry pot can be dramatically lighter. Over time this becomes one of the quickest moisture tests.

Water the whole root ball

Pour slowly over the growing medium rather than giving a tiny splash. Aim to evenly moisten the root zone.

Let excess water escape

Use drainage holes. Let excess water run out, then empty the saucer or decorative outer pot so roots are not sitting in water.

Check again — don't automatically repeat

A reminder saying “water Monstera” should really mean “check Monstera”. If the mix is still appropriately moist, wait.

🧠 Useful watering tricks

☝️

Finger test

Feel below the surface rather than judging by the colour of the top alone.
🏋️

Lift test

Learn how heavy the pot feels wet versus dry.
🥢

Wooden skewer

For deeper pots, insert a clean wooden skewer into the mix. Damp mix tends to leave the skewer darker or with particles attached.
🚿

Sink or shower

For manageable pots, water thoroughly in a sink or shower and allow them to drain before returning them to their decorative pot.
🪴

Drainage holes

Keep plants in draining nursery pots inside decorative cachepots if the decorative pot has no hole.
🌧️

Season matters

Cooler, darker winter conditions often mean slower drying than bright summer conditions.
☀️

Window matters

A sunny, warm window can dry a pot much faster than the same plant across the room.
📓

Track observations

Write down when you checked and watered. You'll quickly learn each plant's rhythm without blindly following dates.

The houseplant survival guide

Light

Full/direct sun: several hours of actual sun on leaves. Great for many succulents and some herbs/fruiting plants.

Bright indirect: lots of daylight but no harsh beam on the leaves. This suits a huge range of foliage plants.

Low light: a bright room away from windows. “Low light” does not mean darkness. No plant can thrive indefinitely in a completely windowless room without suitable grow lighting.

Water

Check the growing medium before watering. Water thoroughly until some drains from the bottom, then empty standing water. Plants in cool rooms generally need less water than plants in warm, bright rooms.

Yellow leaves do not automatically mean “needs water”. Overwatering can also cause yellowing, leaf drop and root problems.

Pot size

Choose a pot with a drainage hole. A useful beginner approach is to pot up only modestly — often one pot size larger when the roots have filled the existing container. Decorative cachepots are fine, but don't let the inner pot sit permanently in water.
